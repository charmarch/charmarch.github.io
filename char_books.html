may 2026

fast food nation

eric schlosser

I'm not particularly big on fast food; I'm a bit of a food snob, and a tad weary of corporate worship. I've been lucky enough to have grown up with delicious home-cooked meals, and have been surrounded by excellent dining options throughout my adolescence and young adulthood. I appreciate some crispy-juicy fried chicken and a Chipotle bowl. I like fries.

In grade eight, our class watched Supersize Me, which was offputting but unsurprising. Later I listened to a podcast that said that Morgan Spurlock's findings were partially inaccurate, as a large, indicting difference before and after his month of McDonald's was the state of his liver -- he had a drinking problem before the documentary started filming. Oh well. I didn't eat much McDonald's nor quit because of Supersize Me anyway.

Fast Food Nation takes a more stomach-churning perspective than an individual's dietary experiment: that of the people on whom such a uniform, ubiquitous industry depends. We travel from the Ford-inspired inventors of the fast food kitchen workflow, to the overworked teenagers who operate the restaurants, to the dwindling farmers of Idaho whose potatoes form the fries, to the cattle ranchers losing land, to the slaughterhouse workers who are lacerated, crushed, stabbed, chemically poisoned to death, and so on. The growth and reach of the fast food industry is monstrous. Schlosser again and again relays that it was with government subsidies and corporate lobbying that the fast food giants gained their footing, and their franchises. Unions were smothered, politicians bought, and dissenters silenced.

One of the less gruesome, yet equally foul, chapters describes the "McLibel" case that the McDonald's Corporation filed in 1990 against two English activists from London Greenpeace, Helen Steel and David Morris. It was revealed that the execs of McDonald's UK had ties to the Metropolitan Police and Scotland Yard, and that the Met Police had shared information with the private investigators hired by McDonald's to infiltrate London Greenpeace. Furthermore, a number of undercover investigators had had intimate relations with some of the activists, including Steel, whose partner of two years at the time of the case was an investigator as well. This breach of respect was on the individual scale. But these tendrils of corporate power, obsessively surveilling and disarming opposition regardless of their influence, climb into every related field: farming, marketing, franchising -- from farm to factory to table to court.

I don't know if having read this book will change my lifestyle majorly -- there's not too much to change from the fast food consumption standpoint -- but it will definitely stick with me. I hope to make thoughtful food and retail purchases that consider both my health and the health of the people whose labour produced them.

april 2026

one by one

freida mcfadden

This book was an interesting foray into the psychological thriller genre. I think my last dip was when I read The Girl on the Train when I was 12 or 13, which at that point was a world away from my standard fare (fantasy YA novels). Backstory for my reading One by One: I went on beach holiday with two of my closest friends, obviously we needed beach reads. I had brought my boyfriend's dad's copy of The Day of the Jackal by Frederick Forsyth, a dense and historically based political thriller about an assassin hired to kill Charles de Gaulle. More on that down here. My friends had picked up two Freida McFaddens, The Inmate and The Teacher, at a bookstore in Prague which they planned to exchange.

The number of gasps and sudden thwaps of books closing to say "oh my god" I heard from these two whilst we basked on our towels..! In one-on-one conversations with me, they gave updates on plot developments (they couldn't spoil their book for the other). And the plots were crazy!!! Toxic, abusive relationships, murderous men and women, and cheating are characteristic elements of a McFadden plot (n=3). My contributions to the conversations were more like:

"The assassin is putting in coloured contacts now!"

or,

"The detectives are trying to get in contact with another government agency!"

which is to say, my selection was considerably drier and slower-paced, not totally to a fault, but this gap in the shock-factor of our books did induce FOMO -- and I am of course Miss FOMO.

One by One was a very fast read. These books are about the length of three or four tanning sessions, and perfect for it. Very blockbuster movielike -- a scene unfolds with little introspection, maybe enough to contextualize a relationship (mostly amounting to "I love my kids" or "I hate my husband"), but most of the text conveys actions and dialogue, and the rest is a hammer beating the nails of relatability into you. In this sense, the writing itself is not my favourite. Here is a sample from a couple short paragraphs:

I'm in the mood for some greasy french fries, but I get overruled and we end up at the diner. I'm still going to get some greasy fries. (…) The entire place smells like grease and burgers. I inhale deeply, already decided I'm going to get a big juicy burger.

WHAT??? Anyway, that tidbit was by far my favourite part of the book. It's a nonsensical, bland start to a chapter, that, in two sentences, says something way more absurd than the premise of the book: that if you want greasy french fries, it is possible to get OVERRULED by everyone deciding to eat at a diner. That's where greasy french fries live????? And the repetition of "grease" and "burgers" kills me too. Amazing. (The premise, by the way, is that some couples who are all friends go for a glamping trip, but the van breaks down on the way to their hotel and they get picked off one by one in the woods. Cheating is involved.)

So it is quite repetitive, and doesn't leave much room for the reader to infer, or like, think about anything (aside from whodunnit and/or how much they want a big juicy burger). But the book did leave breadcrumbs well and ambiguously enough that you and the narrator are lost together. I admired the pacing, the thrill of the plot, the simplicity of the characters, all coming together to get my fingers to turn the page. That said, thanks to McFadden's abundant red herrings, which I know are operating as planned, the twist itself felt like it was pulling my legs beneath me for no reason. Like it was kind of random and I know the whole point is to really gag you but I personally prefer my books to have at least some reread value.

I would not be opposed to another psychological thriller though! I had a lot of fun, and I feel like I know my friends better, having read what they so enjoyed. But maybe just for the beach.

the day of the jackal

frederick forsyth

Borrowed from my boyfriend's bedside table, to read half while he was out and about and half while I was on holiday. The Day of the Jackal is a political thriller published in 1971, about a foreign assassin hired by members of the OAS, a French paramilitary organization, to kill Charles de Gaulle. It starts with a historical basis -- a description of a real attempt on the President's life in August 1962 -- and slowly builds a cat-and-mouse plot from there.

I found the first third (or first half?) quite dull; the exposition was fine, a bit confusing with all the acronyms for government agencies and dissident groups as someone who is unfamiliar with this era of France. It was the painstaking details of the Jackal's preparations for his missions that bored me: measurements of compartments to the inch, the processes of obtaining false documents, various hair tints and contacts, which all pay off, obviously, but good lord. You understand why this book became a some sort of a manual for a wannabe political assassin.

It does pick up eventually, once the preparations are over and the French police catch on. There are some funny characters. I actually did end up enjoying the book a lot, and I'm glad to have read a classic of the genre. Especially afterwards when I read a book of a different calibre and found myself grateful that dense, thoughtful books like this one are beloved.

the virgin suicides

jeffrey eugenides

Tout d’abord, si vous voyez un problème dans le texte, dites-moi; j’apprends le français... Je sais déjà que ma structure syntaxique est plus anglaise que française, et j’essaye de l’améliorer. Donc j’accepte tous types de corrections.

Et maintenant, ma critique:

Je voulais lire The Virgin Suicides depuis trèssss longtemps. Je le voyais toujours chez BMV (une librairie qui n’est pas trop chère) mais le rabais n’était jamais suffisant pour l’acheter. Enfin, je me suis abonnée à la bibliothèque de Montréal et je l’ai trouvé là, avec la conséquence que j’ai dû lire en français…

À part mon vocabulaire faible, c’était une réussite. Il y avait des mots très courants du quotidien parmi les mots plus littéraires que je ne connais pas (par exemple: les buissons)… la honte. Mais c’est le premier livre que je lis en français et en tout cas je suis contente de l'avoir fait.

À propos du livre: je ne sais pas à quoi je m’attendais mais la réalité était différente. En fait j’ai pensé que les filles se tuaient une après l’autre, comme dans un film d’horreur, mais ce n’était pas le cas. C’est plus réaliste, moins dramatique, puis beaucoup plus triste, que j’ai imaginé. Le point de vue des garçons était un choix fascinant — on ne voit presque jamais le point de vue des filles, sauf quand c’est quoi que les garçons ont rêvé, crée, pour essayer de comprendre quelle tragédie a touché leur vie. On parle du voyeurisme, de la culture intime et de la surveillance envahissante de petites villes, de la religion étouffante, des façons dont on peut s’effondrer.

january 2026

the left hand of darkness

ursula k. le guin

It's now the end of May, and I'm trying to write about The Left Hand of Darkness which I read in January. 2026 has been a blur: I finished my degree in December, hibernated through winter, took a French class, went on holiday to London for the first time in March, and romped around Europe in April. And now I'm sitting in my grandma's garden in the suburbs. It is 24 degrees today. When I was reading The Left Hand of Darkness, it was minus 24.

I've had ample time to hone my synopsis since I've been recommending this book to friends since I read it so many moons ago. My pitch goes like this: a diplomat from an interplanetary alliance of humans travels to a planet named Winter to convince the inhabitants to join. Then I try to recapture their attention (it seems my friends don't usually go for sci-fi) by saying that there's a cultural barrier that the diplomat must overcome because the people of Winter are androgynous and only assume a sex maybe once a month for mating purposes. I don't really get into the whole exiled journey across ice part, but I do say it is a political thriller about FRIENDSHIP!

The Left Hand of Darkness was incredible. It's always the ones that have been on my shelf for years!!! I felt a bit dumb while reading it, but I was happy to be. I had the same feeling as when I read East of Eden in high school, that I was engaging with something much bigger and truer than my life. The writing is so refined and efficient, and I want to say prescient, though I know we humans have had the same sorts of power struggles for millenia. But in the loneliness and cold of the winter, having swapped my social media binges for a glut of news feeds about border controls and hateful right-wing rhetoric gaining traction, The Left Hand of Darkness seemed to speak directly to the world I was in. One quote I saved was:

No, I don't mean love, when I say patriotism. I mean fear. The fear of the other. And its expressions are political, not poetical: hate, rivalry, aggression.

november 2024

woman running in the mountains

yūko tsushima

I bought Woman Running in the Mountains in August and carried it with me for months to no avail. I was immediately touched by the first third, which I read hungrily, and then stagnated. There was the aimlessness of it, a reflection of Takiko, the 21 year old unwed mother who doesn’t seem to realize the implications of not terminating her pregnancy until she gives birth; also there was the sense that her solitude was something I possessed. She does a lot of walking and looking at trees and feeling distant from all other things. It’s always sweet and painful to feel with a character whose existence seems conjured just to be alone.

...Except for her son, Akira, and later, Kambayashi, the father of a disabled 10 year old son, who she becomes enamoured with thanks to their connection in raising a child. The majority of the novel is her trying to sustain herself and Akira while living in her abusive household. She needs to work to pay the tuition at Akira’s daycare; the hours that she needs to drop him off and pick him up conflict with her work; she can’t seem to make money at her next job selling cosmetics because she lacks some natural charisma. Despite this, there isn’t a very depressing tone, it’s more matter-of-fact. Flat, maybe, although pleasant. Hope and a greater sense of dimension come in when she starts working at a plant nursery and visits their greenhouses in the mountains.

Woman Running in the Mountains is the most thrilling when Takiko is fantasizing about the distant, icy mountains her mother told her about as a child, and when she joins the lush summer at Misawa Gardens. The book is very sensual, in its focus on sunlight, green, cold, heat, the pleasure Takiko takes from bathing, from hooking up, from holding Akira, from walking. Then, away from the world of sensation, she sometimes envisions herself as being contained in a quartz, trapped away from others. I loved this. I don’t know. It was beautiful. It was lonely, it was a revelation, it was boring, etc…

At the end, desire strikes Takiko, which energizes the book with purpose, finally, to touch. How to embrace your body’s wants? How to resist them?

getting lost

annie ernaux

Finally reading Annie Ernaux and loving the precision. As you may know (you do know, I won’t stop bringing it up), I compiled 12 songs I wrote in the past few years — mostly the last year — mostly about boys, loneliness, my antidotes to loneliness, storytelling, storytelling about boys, the whole lot. Obviously I am a person who does a lot of feeling and thinking about feeling. I can only hope that I can one day identify and communicate a feeling as well as Ernaux. She’s knifelike. I enjoy how seriously she takes desire (well, what else to do with it), I see myself in her openness, I find every entry so interesting and touching. I would love to read her in French next.

It’s a gift from the universe that I read this now, just as I stop reeling from a twin situation (not so much a copy, but with a lot of the same internal conflict, the same desire and disgust and elation and sadness). I was on the train home last Thursday when I decided to put out an album named Mythos about those feelings, the title trying to get at the fact that the stories are anyone’s stories (girl meets boy, someone is devastated, the end), and then on Sunday I went to BMV with Megan and picked up Getting Lost. As soon as I saw the epigraph, “Voglio vivere una favola” (I want to live a fable), I was sold. It’s hard to explain well how directly that speaks to me, I think it makes more sense for me to simply stand next to that inscription and gaze ahead — everything I say and do reflects the notion. But anyway, what a lucky sequence of events...!

I’m dog earing and dog earing. Favourite passage:

Thursday is in a long, long time, and he never calls. I’m going to have to break up with him, my life is just too stupid. Who am I working on this suntan for?

To conclude my wandering post: I walked home from class hungover, sat in the park to read (very beautiful November 1 with some leaves still holding on), and got very sober at:

It starts with a whim, a pure desire for sex, for one night, and ends with blank, speechless pain.

october 2024

the code book

simon singh

I picked this up at BMV after many months of visiting the science section and contemplating it. The shelf is dominated by cosmology, AI, how the universe began, how humanity might end, etc, since those are the enticing topics to common readers (me included. Kind of over it now though — what do I care if the bang was big?!). Very happy that Simon Singh exists, as a result. I’ve expressed dissatisfaction at the popular math books I’ve read, since they don’t go math-y and rigorous enough in my opinion, not that I want to read a textbook for fun, since they’re typically meant for someone who isn’t willingly enrolled in a math dominant degree. Reading The Code Book, I found it approachable and lighthearted, sufficiently thorough - maybe because it was my introduction to cryptography and I can’t critique it as someone can see what it lacks. Really enjoyed the digression into the deciphering of Linear B, found the topic of the Navajo code talkers in WWII to be one of the most interesting parts of the book, of course loved the Turing section… (next I need to find a book on the Entscheidungsproblem…) Was disproportionately excited to start the public key section — learning what RSA stands for was like meeting a celebrity. Thrilling to learn about PGP and Phil Zimmerman and the NSA and FBI being strange as always. Can’t remember which figure in the book it was who said it, but the notion that math was once thought of to be something that will never touch war; obviously not now. I wish the book was twice as long with more detail since it was so fun to read. It was already about 400 pages. MAKE IT 800! Became really annoying at dinner momentarily (was home for the weekend) when I kept trying to steer the conversation back to ciphers. I MUST learn what’s happened in the cryptography world since Singh published The Code Book in 1999.

It’s very well motivated. Singh always returns to the importance of encryption, in a way that gets kind of condescending sometimes. I get it, Mary Queen of Scots was going to die. And all the banks and all the governments are going to fall if Alice and Bob don’t get this gosh darn key distribution problem straight. No, I liked it.

this is your brain on birth control

sarah e. hill, PhD

my dear friend recommended me this some weeks ago, since she knows i'm in the midst of trying to read books about women's (reproductive) health. and in turn i have been recommending this to many people, including my roommate, my mother, my sister, and some friends. sarah hill goes over the ovulation cycle (iirc -- the alternative name for the menstrual cycle) and the role of sex hormones in the body, then talks about the artificial hormones birth control is made of, and how it affects the various systems working to keep the body functioning. it is literally bonkers how it can change a person. my friend, while telling me about the book, mentioned that the cortisol section was pretty crazy, and i agree it's wild how women on hormonal birth control don't release cortisol (or at least not like people who aren't on it).

hill gives a lot of disclaimers on certain effects of birth control on human women -- "there is only a little bit of research suggesting this", "this is true in other primates but hasn't been looked into for humans", "there appears to be a correlation but we can't confirm causation without a lot more rigour", etc, but emphasizes the importance of knowing that such ideas exist and may be true, for her readers making a decision regarding hormonal birth control. and reading all the known effects and very-possible effects made me think "dear god, this is horrifying", but her wrap-up at the end was excellent. she talks about the difficulty in funding medical research on women, because of the difficulty in conducting medical research on women (not just on specifically birth control related matters), and why the effects of birth control on the brain haven't been looked into deeply until recently. and of course talks about how important for women's equality birth control has been. throughout the book her voice is very clear; she comes off as friendly, concerned, intense, silly. i don't know if her style is suited to everyone, but it's definitely approachable for an audience who isn't super well-versed in biology. super glad i read this!

clarice lispector is hard to follow if you like a strict logic in your books. i have a mental list of my friends to whom i would recommend her work -- they happen to be the more delusional ones (said with affection, because me too) -- and those to whom i would recommend it, and then say, "i don't think you'll like it, though".

What am I doing in writing to you? trying to photograph perfume.

Jasmine is for lovers. It makes you want to put an ellipsis now. They walk holding hands, swinging their arms and giving each other gentle kisses to the fragrant almost-sound of jasmine.
i've been reading that passage over for a day now. what lispector and the translator, stefan tobler, create is expressive and mystical. i don't think i've read a better representation of thinking and feeling.

may 2024

symmetry and the monster, DNF

mark ronan

this has been on my reading list since autumn of second year, when a TA for algebra 1 mentioned it. group theory was the sweetest topic that semester to me -- i liked that way of thinking, i really liked symmetry groups, and i got attached to the topic the way you do when you have hours and hours of homework for it every week.

the interweaving of math and history in symmetry and the monster is pretty good, although sometimes jarring. some of the historical tidbits included seem random, such as in the paragraph about lie and klein's visit to paris in 1870. ronan explains that it was cut short by war with prussia breaking out, and that "as winter set in, animals from the zoo were auctioned off for meat". okay????

although it's more of pop science book, with how it's gentle (as in nonrigorous) and introductory, i feel that if i didn't come in knowing some basics i would be very lost. i think, for definitions or explanations, ronan lingers on the obvious and sweeps over the nonintuitive. that being said, i suppose people don't have the same intuition; maybe what i find wildly confusing comes naturally to someone who has spurned mathematics. i recommended it to my dad, so if he gets around to it i'll see what he has to say, because i'm pretty sure he doesn't know what a group is. as well, i do need to state my disclaimer that i never finished this; i started it many times. so i do not speak wholly knowledgeable..

gut symmetries

jeanette winterson

last year, after i finished bluets by maggie nelson, an online friend recommended strongly written on the body by jeanette winterson to me. i noted that down for later, and then later came, and i didn't feel like reading anything. i didn't care about fiction or nonfiction, i didn't want to be introduced to characters or facts that i would have to make room in my brain for.

winter semester came, which is my enemy. i slept. i went on my phone. i did my schoolwork with great reluctance. i did not read.

then i had a hell schedule of exams and a budding spring romance -- i survived the first and have been trying to sustain the second -- and finally made it out into the real world (the bookstore) with my sister. i've browsed the jeannette winterson titles in this bookstore before, considered this one, put it back, but this time i decided to purchase it. the sciencey synopsis appealed to me. also the idea of a love triangle.

gut symmetries didn't end up being much of either, to me. i think my issue (not with the book, but as a reader) is that when i want science i want it difficult and real. i want a textbook, with emotional annotations. in first year, when i was taking linear algebra, i couldn't let go of the idea of using vectors to represent how digital or epistolary communication is a flattening: a projection onto a plane. it was a very literal, bijective metaphor, and that's what i was craving with this book. there's some of that, but it gets drowned beneath the prose. felt like having to wade through data to uncover significant results. i keep feeling like i should do a disclaimer: for literate people who enjoy literary fiction and the like, this is probably excellent. i've been feeling quite illiterate lately and would enjoy reading a function defined f(alice, who is the main narrator) = f(stella, the wife of jove) = f(jove, who alice is having an affair with). actually i think f(a) = f(s) != f(j). not sure. doing silly made up math reminded me of this part where alice (i think) is saying that her and stella and jove didn't add up or multiply or something like that; you don't get 1 + 2 = 3, or maybe it was something else. it was her meditating on the fact that their combination mathematically/practically was unexpected. girl that's a permutation. a combination even. it was literally her being like 3 choose 2. i just wanted to be like ok well you just need to choose the right equation. cuz i'm annoying

more concisely, here are my notes after i finished:

  • shallowly suited to me: science metaphors, tarot cards, love triangle
  • not dense writing but intricate for my taste. indirect and obfuscated. prose was nice but not that nice to me
  • lots of water. formless, can be shaped, can drown in, need to live, boats for life?

  • eating flesh. as always. i liked this part
  • alice pov at the end where she describes her emotions and how they are guards against lovers i really loved that a lot

one last thing: i sent a pic of a page (it had quotes from einstein and oppenheimer, obviously chosen and placed to be thinly veiled comments on alice's viewpoint and emotional state) to someone i was seeing at the time, and asked him what he thought of science metaphors for human connection and such. he said (paraphrased), "yeah sure, as long people understand the actual meaning", which i'm inclined to disagree with, because i think you don't need to understand quantum physics to understand that a person is both a particle and a wave, but then i didn't think either of us were talking about the same thing. or maybe he couldn't wrap his head around feeling...

she comes first

ian kerner, PhD

one night on instagram dms, my best friend sent me a video of herself talking about a book that she was reading as a research project, in a way. she comes first is a book written by a man, primarily for men, about giving head to women. as she is a girl in a relationship with a girl, she was curious about the perspective and how much of it she would already be familiar with. i think her review partway through was something like "i was surprised how much of what is intuitive to me was written out explicitly, but there were a few things i learned". since i need to know what's going on in her mind 24/7 (she's like my own personal influencer) i decided to also borrow it from the library.

what ensued was 48 hours of me spamming her texts with various screenshots of the book with commentary. some highlights were:

  • ian kerner's brief list of vulva-wine pairings
  • ian kerner's warning that blowing up there may result in an embolism which may result in death, immediately followed by p***y-eating affirmations (he calls them the Three Assurances)
  • the chapter title "Think Outside Her Box"
  • ian kerner's extensive list of erotica he recommends to read with your partner which includes delta of venus by anaïs nin which is a book i would not read with another soul if my life depended on it
  • A LOLITA QUOTE.
  • the ending line being "When she comes first, she comes forever."
needless to say, this book incited a lot of emotions in me such as fear, anxiety, delight, amusement, and shock (vulva-wine pairings is crazyyyy). i think i talked about it to like six people just to bring up the wine pairings. none of them seemed that keen to read it after that, but honestly it was an informative and fun read. and i'm not really in the position of needing or wanting to study it, so it was a one and done ordeal for me.

was it really a one and done ordeal for me, though? it spiked so much anxiety in me that i decided i needed to read a book about female sexuality from a female perspective. not so much as a palate cleanser, but as an assurance. not that she comes first was wrong, but that i got so weird about it all being super goal-oriented even with its occasional disclaimer that it should be pleasure-oriented that i was like telling my best friend "oh my god i need an affirmation". so thank you, ian kerner, for inspiring my summer of books about sexuality and the body.

a final note: what my best friend was saying about what was intuitive to her being written out explicitly, i agree with. kerner very clearly describes the process of female arousal, how long it typically takes, et cetera, that she comes first was an unspoken lesson on male arousal as an inverse.

come as you are

emily nagoski

i heard about come as you are by emily nagoski several years ago from a youtube video by hannah witton, a sex educator. this was where i first encountered the framework nagoski introduces in come as you are, that of sexual inhibitors and accelerators and the importance of context to (especially female) sexuality. a little while later, i watched a video featuring nagoski herself explaining this, which stuck it in my brain a bit better, although i never really thought about it again until i was reading she comes first by ian kerner and somewhat frantically decided i needed to read a book about female sexuality from a female author.

come as you are is fine. it's great, actually, i just think that i'm not the bull's-eye-of-the-target audience, that is, people who want/need to hear affirmation after affirmation about how beautiful and normal they are. it was definitely nice to hear, but the constant reiteration became grating. and there were so many metaphors about how to think of your sexuality and body and i am just really not a person who needs to consider any part of me as a garden so that was a little troublesome. i guess i'm fortunate enough that society hasn't knocked my (apparently large) ego down that many pegs?

i'm only half serious about that, though, since i am still in the target audience (just maybe not bull's eye). i did actually learn a lot about spontaneous vs. responsive desire, arousal, how to enable it, a billion other things that all started to blend together until the only thing i retained was, "everyone is different. everyone is normal. you can change the context." i would recommend this book to all my female friends, or at least the worksheets included at the end of sections. maybe check out the tl;dr's at the end of the sections instead. because i did get quite bored even while learning things.

bonus text i sent my best friend when i was done:

Tediously repetitive about how beautiful and normal everyone is but i recognize that a lot of people really do need to hear that bc they don’t already have an ego the size of the moon. I could do without all the garden metaphors and the like. I do think it’s a valuable read because of how much it tries to dismantle common notions of women’s sexuality and what it should be, including those that are puritanical and consider it like sinful but also those that are “modern” and idealize needlessly porn-like sex.
